# Choosing the option the hypotheses actually prove

You will see a research-level mathematics question with five options, exactly one of which is correct. The options are often close to each other: several may be true statements, several may look like the result in question, and the wrong ones usually differ from the right one in a single detail. Work through the steps below before you answer.

## 1. Pin down the setting
- List the hypotheses the question gives: the objects, their dimensions or parameters, the assumptions (smoothness, compactness, characteristic, positivity, range of an exponent, and so on).
- Note what is being asked for: an existence statement, a bound, an exact value, an equivalence, a classification.

## 2. Read each option as a precise claim
For every option, write down in one line:
- its quantifiers (for all / there exists, and in which order);
- any constants and whether they are uniform or may depend on other parameters;
- boundary and extremal cases (strict vs. non-strict inequality, endpoints included or not, "at most" vs. "exactly");
- the scope it claims (which class of objects, which range of parameters).

## 3. Test derivability first
Ask of each option one question: does it follow from the hypotheses as stated?
- Reject an option that needs an assumption the question does not grant, or that claims more generality, uniformity or sharpness than the hypotheses support.
- Reject an option that is about a different object or a different question, even if it is a true and familiar fact.
- Check a small or degenerate case (lowest dimension, smallest parameter, boundary value) against each surviving option; one counterexample removes it.

## 4. Only then compare strength
If more than one option survives step 3, prefer the one that answers exactly what was asked with the strongest conclusion the hypotheses still justify. A weaker statement that is merely implied by the answer is not the answer; a stronger statement the hypotheses cannot reach is not either.

## 5. Meta-options about stronger results
- Treat options of the form “one of the remaining options is correct, but a stronger result can be proven” as serious candidates, especially when the question asks for the strongest statement.
- If a concrete option is true but your theorem or derivation gives a strictly stronger conclusion not exactly listed, choose the meta-option rather than the weaker concrete statement.

## 6. Answer
State the letter of the option you chose, in the format the question asks for. Do not leave the answer implicit.
